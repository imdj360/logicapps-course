<Step id="problem" name="A growing backlog" tag="PROBLEM">
Every night, 50,000 invoice messages land on a queue. One worker would take until noon. And among them are a few dozen **urgent** credit notes that shouldn't wait.
</Step>

<Step id="pattern" name="Competing consumers">
Microsoft's **Competing Consumers** pattern: several consumer instances read from the **same queue**, and each message goes to **only one** of them. Add consumers when the backlog grows.

It's the opposite of publish-subscribe, where **every** subscriber gets every message.
</Step>

<Step id="logicapps" name="In Logic Apps">
In Logic Apps, our mapping: a workflow with the built-in **peek-lock** Service Bus trigger is the consumer. As a Workflow Service Plan scales out, more instances read from the queue at once.

Microsoft's Functions example shows that peek-lock **hides** a message from other consumers while one is working on it; on success it's completed, on failure it can be abandoned.
</Step>

<Step id="idempotent" name="Order and duplicates" tag="PROBLEM">
Two consequences of many workers:

- **Order isn't guaranteed** across consumers. If order matters, Service Bus sessions can give FIFO ordering (our note: the sequential convoy lesson shows how).
- A queue delivers each message **at least once**, so a message can be processed twice. Microsoft's advice: process messages **idempotently**.
</Step>

<Step id="poison" name="Poison messages">
A malformed message can fail on every worker that tries it. Service Bus moves a message to the **dead-letter queue** once its delivery count passes **MaxDeliveryCount**, so it can't cycle forever. Our suggestion: a separate workflow on the dead-letter queue to alert and repair.
</Step>

<Step id="priority" name="Priority queues">
Microsoft's **Priority Queue** pattern gives urgent work its own path. Its Service Bus example: send to a **topic** with a **Priority** property, and use **SQL filters** to route high and low priority to separate **subscriptions**, each with its own consumers, more of them for high priority.

Priority queues can also be a single queue ordered by priority, or separate queues per priority.
</Step>

<Step id="starvation" name="Don't starve the rest" tag="TIP">
With one consumer pool favouring the high-priority queue, low-priority messages can be continually delayed and potentially never processed. Microsoft says to consider **dynamically raising the priority** of old messages, if possible; it also describes dedicated consumer pools per priority as an option.
</Step>

<Step id="when" name="When to use them">
Competing consumers fit work that's **independent**, can run **asynchronously** and comes in **variable volumes**. They don't fit tasks that must run in sequence or depend heavily on each other.

Priority queues fit different service levels, such as premium and standard customers, but not work where strict first-in-first-out matters more.
</Step>

<Step id="recap" name="Recap" tag="RECAP">
- **Competing consumers**: many workers, one queue, each message to one worker.
- In Logic Apps: peek-lock trigger, scaled out by the plan.
- No ordering guarantee and at-least-once delivery: design **idempotently**.
- **MaxDeliveryCount** sends poison messages to the dead-letter queue.
- **Priority**: topic plus SQL filters, with dedicated consumers per level.
</Step>
