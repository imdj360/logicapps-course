<Step id="problem" name="No rollback" tag="PROBLEM">
An order goes through three systems: **reserve stock**, **charge the card**, **book the courier**. The courier booking fails. Stock is reserved and the card is charged, in two systems that already committed their changes.

Microsoft's point: traditional ACID guarantees aren't directly applicable across **independently managed data stores**. There's nothing to roll back.
</Step>

<Step id="saga" name="The saga pattern">
A **saga** is a sequence of **local transactions**: each step completes its work in one service and triggers the next. If a step fails, **compensating transactions** undo the changes that the completed steps made.

For our order: release the stock, refund the card.
</Step>

<Step id="types" name="Three kinds of step">
Microsoft names three:

- **Compensable**: can be undone by another transaction with the opposite effect, like reserving stock.
- **Pivot**: the **point of no return**, like shipping the parcel.
- **Retryable**: **idempotent** steps after the pivot, so they can be retried safely until the saga reaches its final state.

Microsoft's advice on irreversible steps: do them only after all critical validations succeed. Our reading: put the risky, compensable steps before the pivot.
</Step>

<Step id="orchestration" name="Orchestration or choreography">
- **Choreography**: services exchange events, with no central controller. Good for simple flows with few services.
- **Orchestration**: a central **orchestrator** tells each participant what to do. Better for complex flows, but the orchestrator is a point of failure.

In our view, a stateful Logic Apps workflow is a natural orchestrator. Microsoft's Scheduler Agent Supervisor page lists Logic Apps as an option for its Scheduler role, for visual workflows and connector-based integration.
</Step>

<Step id="compensate" name="Compensate in Logic Apps">
Our design: run each step in order inside a **Try** scope, and record which steps completed. A **Catch** scope that runs on failure or timeout then calls the compensation for each completed step: refund, then release.

Microsoft notes compensation doesn't have to run in exact reverse order, and doesn't necessarily restore the exact starting state: a refund isn't the same as never charging.
</Step>

<Step id="fails" name="When compensation fails" tag="PROBLEM">
Microsoft is blunt: compensating transactions **can fail too**. Its advice:

- Make every compensation step **idempotent**, so it can be retried safely.
- **Record progress**, so compensation can resume from where it failed.
- When nothing else works, **alert a person**: manual intervention is sometimes the only recovery.
</Step>

<Step id="when" name="When to use it">
Use a saga when a business operation spans several services or stores and must be undone if a later step fails.

Microsoft's Saga page says it may not suit **tightly coupled** transactions; the Compensating Transaction page adds: skip compensation when **retries alone** are enough, or when the system **can't tolerate temporary inconsistency** (our gloss: between the failure and the compensation, the data is briefly wrong).
</Step>

<Step id="recap" name="Recap" tag="RECAP">
- Across services there's **no rollback**: compensate instead.
- A **saga** is local transactions plus compensations.
- Steps are **compensable**, **pivot** or **retryable**.
- In our design, Logic Apps is the **orchestrator**: Try, record progress, Catch and compensate.
- Compensations must be **idempotent**, resumable and alert-backed.
</Step>
