<Step id="problem" name="Bursts in, steady out" tag="PROBLEM">
Orders arrive in bursts: hundreds in a minute after a promotion, then nothing for an hour. The ERP can only take them at its own steady pace.

A common answer, and the one this lesson uses, is a **Service Bus queue** between them: the shop puts orders on the queue, and a workflow takes them off as fast as the ERP allows.
</Step>

<Step id="connectors" name="Built-in or managed">
Standard has two Service Bus connectors. The **built-in** one, under **Built-in**, is service provider based and available only in Standard. The **managed** one is under **Shared**.

Microsoft says the built-in connector generally provides better performance, capabilities and pricing. It also handles **large messages**, while the managed connector is limited to **1 MB** per message, even on a premium namespace.
</Step>

<Step id="trigger" name="The queue trigger">
The built-in trigger **When messages are available in a queue** can return **one or more** messages per run.

By default it **auto-completes**: in a stateful workflow, once the trigger's output is saved, the trigger completes the message. If a failure happens, it abandons the message. In a stateless workflow, the decision is made only after the run completes.
</Step>

<Step id="peeklock" name="Peek-lock: you decide">
With **When messages are available in a queue (peek-lock)**, the message stays on the queue, **locked**, while your workflow works on it. Your workflow then settles it, for example with **Complete**, **Abandon** or **Dead-letter** (there's also Defer), using the message's lock token.

The lock doesn't last forever. Our advice: keep runs shorter than the lock (when you tune trigger concurrency and waiting runs, Microsoft warns that runs shouldn't wait longer than the message lock timeout), or use **Renew lock on a message in queue**.
</Step>

<Step id="sessions" name="In order: sessions">
When related messages must be processed **in order**, such as all updates for one order, give them the same **session ID** and use a session-enabled queue. Microsoft calls this the **sequential convoy** pattern.

On the built-in trigger, set **IsSessionsEnabled** to **Yes**. Session actions let you get messages from a session, complete or abandon them, and renew or close the session. The connector caches up to **1,500 sessions** per messaging entity.
</Step>

<Step id="deadletter" name="The dead-letter queue">
Messages can be moved to the queue's **dead-letter queue**, for example by your workflow's **Dead-letter** action.

To read them, point a built-in queue trigger at `your-queue/$deadletterqueue`. That gives you a second workflow to alert, repair or resubmit.
</Step>

<Step id="concurrency" name="Concurrency and batches" tag="TIP">
If the trigger returns batches and you set **trigger concurrency**, keep the concurrent runs **larger than the batch size**. With concurrency on, there's a **30-second** delay between batch reads by default.

<Callout title="Watch out">On the **(V1)** queue trigger, enabling concurrency and sending 100 or more messages sends **all** messages to the dead-letter queue.</Callout>
</Step>

<Step id="auth" name="Connecting">
The built-in connector authenticates with a **connection string**, **Microsoft Entra ID (OAuth)** or a **managed identity**. Use a connection string for the **namespace**: if it contains `EntityPath`, it's for a specific entity and isn't the right one.

And avoid an infinite loop: a trigger and an action that use the **same queue** can feed each other forever.
</Step>

<Step id="recap" name="Recap" tag="RECAP">
- Prefer the **built-in** Service Bus connector in Standard: better performance, and large messages (managed caps at 1 MB).
- The default trigger **auto-completes**; **peek-lock** lets you Complete, Abandon or Dead-letter.
- **Sessions** keep related messages in order (sequential convoy).
- Read failures from `queue/$deadletterqueue`.
- Keep concurrency above the batch size, and use a namespace-level connection.
</Step>
